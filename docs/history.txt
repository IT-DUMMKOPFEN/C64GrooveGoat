# The History of C64 SID Music

The MOS Technology SID (Sound Interface Device), type MOS 6581 and later MOS/CSG 8580, is one of
the most remarkable sound chips of early home computing. It was designed in 1981 by Bob Yannes
and made the Commodore 64 (C64, 1982) not just a home computer with a beeper, but a real
synthesizer. This chapter tells its story - the chip, its two versions, the composers who made it
famous and what remains of it today.

# 1. What made the SID so special?

Before the C64, sound in home computers was mostly an afterthought: simple square waves, clicks
and beeps (like the speaker of the Apple II or the IBM PC). Bob Yannes, an engineer at MOS
Technology and a fan of music synthesizers, wanted to build a real synthesizer on a single chip.
He and his team designed the SID in about five months in the second half of 1981. Yannes left
Commodore in 1983 and co-founded the synthesizer company Ensoniq.

## The architecture

- Three independent voices. Each oscillator covers the range from almost 0 Hz up to about 4 kHz
  (roughly 8 octaves), set by a 16-bit frequency value.
- Four waveforms per voice: sawtooth, triangle, pulse with variable pulse width (12 bit) and
  noise. Triangle, sawtooth and pulse can also be selected together; the chip then produces
  "combined waveforms" with their own, characteristic sound.
- One ADSR envelope generator per voice (attack, decay, sustain, release), so that the volume of
  a note can follow the shape of real instruments: drums, plucked strings, brass, pads.
- A programmable multimode analog filter with low-pass, band-pass and high-pass outputs (also
  combined, e.g. a notch filter), adjustable cutoff frequency and resonance. Each voice can be
  routed through the filter or not.
- Oscillator synchronization and ring modulation between the voices for metallic, bell-like and
  evolving sounds.
- A master volume of 16 steps, two paddle (potentiometer) inputs and an audio input.

## Tricks and hacks

Musicians and programmers soon learned to go beyond the limits of the chip:

- Arpeggios: by changing the pitch of a voice on every frame of the screen (50 times per second
  on PAL, 60 times on NTSC computers), one voice can play a chord as a fast series of notes. This
  "C64 arpeggio" became the signature sound of the platform.
- Wave and pulse programs: changing the waveform and the pulse width from frame to frame
  (for example noise for one frame, then pulse with falling notes) creates drums and complex
  instrument sounds - the principle of the wavetables in GoatTracker and C64 GrooveGoat.
- Digitized samples: writing the 4-bit master volume register produces small steps of the output
  level, because of a DC offset in the 6581. Writing it thousands of times per second plays 4-bit
  samples - speech, drums and short digitized sounds.
- Multiplexing: good drivers let one voice alternate between bass, drum and melody notes, so
  that three voices sound like more.

# 2. MOS 6581 and MOS 8580

Commodore produced two main versions of the SID. Because large parts of the chip are analog, they
sound clearly different.

                         +---------------------------------+
                         |         SID CHIP FAMILY         |
                         +----------------+----------------+
                                          |
                  +-----------------------+-----------------------+
                  |                                               |
      +-----------+-----------+                       +-----------+-----------+
      |    MOS 6581 (NMOS)    |                       |  MOS 8580 (HMOS-II)   |
      |  original C64         |                       |  later C64C, C64G,    |
      |  1982 - about 1986    |                       |  C128DCR, from 1986   |
      +-----------+-----------+                       +-----------+-----------+
                  |                                               |
      12 V supply voltage                             9 V supply voltage
      non-linear filter that differs                  linear filter, the same
      from chip to chip                               on every chip, strong resonance
      warm, rough, "dirty" sound                      clean, bright, precise sound
      loud volume-register samples                    samples almost inaudible

## MOS 6581 ("old SID")

- Used in the original C64 from 1982 and made until about 1986.
- NMOS process with a 12 V supply.
- Sound: warm, full, slightly distorted.
- The filter varies a lot from chip to chip because of manufacturing tolerances: the same song
  sounds brighter on one C64 and darker on another. Many composers wrote for "their" chip.
- The DC offset makes volume-register samples loud and clear.
- Combined waveforms are quiet and sound different from the 8580.

## MOS 8580 ("new SID")

- Used in newer C64 models from about 1986/87 (later C64C boards, C64G) and in the C128DCR.
- HMOS-II process with a 9 V supply; runs cooler and is more robust.
- Sound: clean, bright and precise. The filter is linear and behaves the same on every chip, and
  its resonance is much stronger.
- Combined waveforms are louder and clearer.
- The DC offset was largely removed, so samples played with the volume register are nearly
  silent. Players and demos use special software techniques or a small hardware modification to
  make them audible again.

C64 GrooveGoat lets you choose the emulated chip (6581 or 8580) in the settings bar; the
instrument library has its own set for each chip, and the random instrument generator adapts its
filter settings to the chip.

# 3. Legendary composers

Early C64 music had to be programmed in 6502 assembly language, usually with a sound driver
written by the musician. The great SID composers were programmers and composers at the same
time.

## Rob Hubbard

Hubbard brought rock and funk structures, driving basslines, fast arpeggios and expressive pitch
bends to game music, and is often called the most influential C64 composer.

- Monty on the Run (1985) - based on Charles Williams' "Devil's Galop", one of the first tunes
  with pitch bends in his driver.
- Commando (1985/86) - a fast, driving theme based on a motif of the arcade game.
- International Karate (1986) - a pentatonic theme inspired by Ryuichi Sakamoto's "Forbidden
  Colours" ("Merry Christmas, Mr. Lawrence").

## Martin Galway

Galway, from Belfast in Northern Ireland, wrote long and complex pieces for Ocean and Imagine and
was one of the pioneers of sampled sounds in SID music.

- Comic Bakery (1984) - one of the best known and most remixed C64 melodies.
- Rambo: First Blood Part II (1985) - a long, orchestral-style soundtrack.
- Wizball (1987) and Arkanoid (1987) - Arkanoid's music is famous for its sampled sounds.

## Chris Hülsbeck

A German composer (born 1968) who wrote catchy, melodic game music and his own music tools.

- Shades (1986) - won the music competition of the German magazine 64'er and started his career.
- Soundmonitor - his music editor, published as a listing in 64'er; one of the first trackers
  for the C64.
- The Great Giana Sisters (1987) - one of the best known melodies of the C64 era.
- Later he wrote the TFMX music system and many famous soundtracks on the Amiga.

## Ben Daglish

Daglish (1966-2018) combined folk melodies, classical structures and fast arpeggios.

- Gauntlet (1986) and Deflektor (1987) - two of his most popular game themes.
- The Last Ninja (1987, together with Anthony Lees) - an atmospheric soundtrack for one of the
  best-selling C64 games.
- Trap (1986) - known for its fast pulse width modulation.

Many more composers shaped the sound of the C64, among them Jeroen Tel, Tim Follin, David
Whittaker, Fred Gray, Jonathan Dunn, Matt Gray and Richard Joseph.

# 4. From assembly to trackers

In the first years every composer wrote music directly in assembly language or with a private
editor. Later, music editors made SID music accessible to everybody:

- Soundmonitor (Chris Hülsbeck) - an early tracker-like editor, published in 64'er.
- JCH Editor (Jens-Christian Huus) and DMC - Demo Music Creator (Brian of Graffity) - the
  standard editors of the demoscene for many years.
- GoatTracker by Lasse Öörni (Cadaver) - a cross-platform tracker for PC, Mac and Linux that
  exports music for the real C64. Its version 2 and the "Silver Fork" by Joel Ricci are the basis
  of C64 GrooveGoat.
- SID-Wizard (Hermit) - a modern tracker that runs on the C64 itself.

The principles are the same as in the 1980s: patterns with notes, instruments made of ADSR values
and small wave, pulse and filter programs - but today they can be edited comfortably and played
with an accurate emulation such as reSID.

# 5. Legacy and preservation

The sound of the SID lives on in modern electronic music:

- High Voltage SID Collection (HVSC): an archive of more than 50,000 SID music files, playable
  with emulators and SID players on any computer. C64 GrooveGoat exports in the PSID format of
  the HVSC.
- Hardware synthesizers with real SID chips, such as the Elektron SidStation and the TherapSID by
  Twisted Electrons.
- Modern replacements and emulations for C64 computers whose SID has failed: SwinSID, ARMSID,
  FPGASID, SIDKick pico, BackSID and others.
- The demoscene still releases new SID compositions every year and keeps discovering new sounds
  and tricks - often with several SID chips, as C64 GrooveGoat supports with up to 8 SIDs.
